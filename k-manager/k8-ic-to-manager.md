# Moving from individual contributor to manager

## In one minute

Management is a change of job, not a promotion within the same one. My results would come
through other people, my calendar would fill with conversations, and I would have to stop
being the person who solves the hardest technical problem. An interviewer wants to hear
that I understand this, that I want it for a sound reason, and that I already show some
of the behaviours.

## Key ideas

- **What changes.**
  - Output: from my own work to the team's work.
  - Time: from long focused blocks to meetings, one-to-ones and interruptions.
  - Feedback: slower and less clear; success is others succeeding.
  - Skills: listening, delegating, deciding with incomplete information, difficult
    conversations.
- **What to stop.** Taking the most interesting task; fixing others' work myself;
  being the technical bottleneck.
- **What to keep.** Enough technical depth to judge quality, ask good questions and
  earn respect; reading code and analyses, not writing most of them.
- **Good reasons to want it.** Wanting more impact than one person can have; enjoying
  others' growth; caring how the team and the business connect.
- **Weak reasons.** Pay, status, or "the next step". There is a senior technical track
  too, and it is an equally valid path.
- **Leadership style.** Describe it in behaviours: clear expectations, context instead
  of control, direct feedback, visible fairness. Adapt to the person and the task.
- **First 90 days.** Listen: one-to-ones with everyone, understand the work, the
  stakeholders and the pain; then a small number of visible improvements; no
  reorganisation in month one.
- **Common first-time manager mistakes.** Doing instead of delegating; avoiding hard
  conversations; over-promising upwards; neglecting one-to-ones when busy.
- **Managing former peers and people more experienced than me.** Be open about it, use
  their expertise, decide where it is my call.
- **Player-coach.** Small teams often need a manager who still does hands-on work; keep
  my own tasks off the critical path.
- **Support.** A mentor, peers who manage, and asking the team for feedback.

## My evidence

No direct reports yet, and I say so. What I can show:

- Teaching and assisting 200+ students, and supporting capstone projects: explaining,
  assessing and coaching.
- Advisory work with vendors: influence without authority.
- Writing coding standards and documentation: raising the level of others' work.
- Measuring business impact: knowing what the team's value rests on.
- Working with the business team in scrum: stakeholder contact.

## Likely questions

1. **Why do you want to manage?** My real reason, in two sentences.
2. **You have never had direct reports. Why should we trust you with a team?** The
   evidence above, and how I would learn.
3. **What will you miss, and how will you cope?** Hands-on depth; keep a small technical
   task off the critical path.
4. **What is your leadership style?** In behaviours, with an example.
5. **What would you do in your first 90 days?** Listen, map, then one visible fix.
